Chapter 1 · Operations efficiency

Operations ratios and the cash conversion cycle

How many days is the company's cash tied up between paying for materials and collecting from customers?

Take Chapter 1 quiz
Profit margin = Net income ÷ Sales
Asset turnover = Sales ÷ Total assets
Receivables turnover = Sales ÷ Avg receivables
Days sales outstanding = 365 ÷ Receivables turnover
Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Avg inventory
Days of inventory = 365 ÷ Inventory turnover
Cash conversion cycle = Days of inventory + DSO − Payable period

Read it as a timeline. Inventory sits for some days, then you wait for the customer to pay. Your supplier lets you pay late, which takes days off the front. What's left is how long your own cash is stuck.

Inventory turnover uses cost of goods sold, not sales. Receivables turnover uses sales. Swapping them is the most common mistake.

Chapter 2 · Strategy

Productivity: partial, multifactor, total

How much output do we get for each dollar of input, and did it get better or worse this year?

Take Chapter 2 quiz
Productivity = Output ÷ Input
Partial = Output ÷ one input (e.g. Labour)
Multifactor = Output ÷ (two or more inputs)
Total = Output ÷ All inputs
% change = (This year − Last year) ÷ Last year

The name depends only on the denominator. One input is partial, some inputs is multifactor, every input is total. Tick inputs on the right and watch the label change.

Labour productivity can go up while total productivity goes down. That happens when another input, often materials, grew faster than output. Exams love asking you to explain it.

Chapter 5 · Projects

PERT three-time estimates

Take Chapter 5 quiz

When an activity's time is uncertain, what time do we plan for, and how uncertain is it?

Expected time ET = (a + 4m + b) ÷ 6
Variance σ² = ((b − a) ÷ 6)²

a = optimistic, m = most likely, b = pessimistic. The most likely time gets 4 times the weight, so ET sits near m but gets pulled toward the long tail.

Variance only cares about the spread between a and b. Move m and the variance doesn't change.

Expected time is not the simple average (a + m + b) ÷ 3. Divide by 6 after weighting m by 4.

Chapter 5 · Projects

Probability of finishing by a deadline

Take Chapter 5 quiz

What are the chances the project is done by week D? Or, flipped: what deadline can we promise with 95% confidence?

Project ET = Σ ET on the critical path
Project σ² = Σ σ² on the critical path
Z = (D − Project ET) ÷ √(Σ σ²)
Deadline D = Project ET + Z × √(Σ σ²)

Z counts standard deviations. Look Z up in the normal table to get the probability. A deadline equal to ET gives Z = 0, which is only a 50% chance.

Add the variances, then take the square root. Adding standard deviations gives the wrong answer. Use only critical-path activities.

Chapter 5 · Projects

Critical path: forward and backward pass

Take Chapter 5 quiz

What's the shortest time the project can take, and which activities can't slip at all?

Forward ES = largest EF of predecessors; EF = ES + t
Backward LF = smallest LS of successors; LS = LF − t
Slack = LS − ES = LF − EF
Critical path = activities with slack 0 = the longest path

Forward pass, take the max: you can't start until every predecessor is done. Backward pass, take the min: you must finish in time for the earliest successor.

Each box shows ES | EF on top and LS | LF underneath. Change a duration and see whether the critical path moves.

At a merge (two arrows into one box), forward pass takes the larger EF. At a burst (one box feeding two), backward pass takes the smaller LS.

Chapter 5 · Projects

Crashing: the time–cost trade-off

Take Chapter 5 quiz

Is it worth paying to finish early? Keep shortening while each week costs less than the overhead it saves.

Crash cost / week = (Crash cost − Normal cost) ÷ (Normal time − Crash time)
Total cost = Direct (activity) cost + Indirect (overhead) cost

The rule: shorten one week at a time. Pick the cheapest activity on the critical path. Stop when the next week costs more than the overhead it saves.

Each week costs more than the one before. You use up the cheap activities first, and once a second path becomes critical, you have to shorten both paths together.

The cheapest activity in the table is useless if it's not on the critical path. Shortening it doesn't make the project any shorter.

Chapter 6 · Manufacturing processes

Little's law

Take Chapter 6 quiz

How much work is sitting in the process, how fast does it come out, and how long does each unit spend inside?

Inventory = Throughput rate × Flow time
Flow time = Inventory ÷ Throughput rate
Throughput = Inventory ÷ Flow time

Cover the one you want in the triangle. What's left tells you to multiply (side by side) or divide (one over the other).

Units must match. If throughput is per hour, flow time must be in hours. Convert minutes before you plug in.

Chapter 6 · Manufacturing processes

Break-even and choosing a process

Take Chapter 6 quiz

How many units before a process makes money, and at what volume does the expensive machine pay off?

Break-even Q = Fixed cost ÷ (Price − Variable cost)
Indifference Q = (F₂ − F₁) ÷ (V₁ − V₂)
Profit = (Price − V) × Q − F

Price − variable cost is what each unit contributes toward fixed costs. Break-even is how many units it takes to cover them.

Indifference point: below it, pick the low fixed-cost process. Above it, the high fixed-cost, low variable-cost process wins.

Price doesn't appear in the indifference formula. You're comparing the two cost lines with each other, not with revenue.

Chapter 6 · Manufacturing processes

Assembly line balancing

Take Chapter 6 quiz

How fast must the line run to meet demand, how many stations do we need, and how much time is wasted?

Cycle time C = Production time per day ÷ Required output per day
Min. stations Nₜ = Σ task times ÷ C (round up)
Efficiency = Σ task times ÷ (Actual stations Nₐ × C)
Idle time = Nₐ × C − Σ task times (per cycle)

C is the time limit for every station. No station's total can go over the dashed line. The tallest bar is the bottleneck, and it sets how fast the line actually runs.

Convert the production time to the same unit as task times (usually seconds). Always round Nₜ up: 4.2 stations means 5.
